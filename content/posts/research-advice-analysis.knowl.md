This qualitative comparison draws on three essays about doing research. Their advice offers questions to consider when choosing problems, learning mathematics, and explaining ideas.

## Choose a problem with an approach

A problem's potential impact is only part of the choice. Ask whether there is a plausible way to attack it: a useful example, an available method, or a smaller question that would make progress possible. A famous unsolved problem is not automatically a productive starting point.

## Develop the tools to make progress

Mathematical research draws on sustained work, education, patience, and learning from others. The image of a solitary genius making effortless discoveries obscures this cumulative work. Talent alone does not replace understanding the details or building on existing mathematics.

## Help others understand

Communicating mathematics involves more than presenting a formally correct proof. Examples, explanations, and conversation help people develop the understanding needed to use a result. What works among specialists may leave a wider audience without the background needed to follow the argument.

## Using this advice

These are reflections by individual researchers. Treat them as prompts for discussing your own work, not as measured predictors of research success.

## References

- Richard Hamming, [*You and Your Research*](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html), discussion of important problems and reasonable attacks.
- Terence Tao, [*Does one have to be a genius to do maths?*](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/does-one-have-to-be-a-genius-to-do-maths/), opening discussion of research habits and collective progress.
- William Thurston, [*On proof and progress in mathematics*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9404236), §§1 and 3 on mathematical understanding and communication.
